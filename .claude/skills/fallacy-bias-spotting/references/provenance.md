# Provenance and Scope

This skill is a screening procedure assembled from established work on argumentation and judgment. The catalog is an editorial selection, not a canonical list, and the cue-phrase heuristic is an implementation detail added for agent use — no source prescribes it.

## Core sources

**Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases." *Science*, 185(4157), 1124–1131.** The founding paper for the bias half: availability, representativeness, and anchoring-and-adjustment as fast substitutions that are usually useful and predictably wrong. Anchoring, availability, and regression to the mean in the catalog trace here.

**Kahneman, D. (2011). *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.** The accessible synthesis: System 1 / System 2, overconfidence, planning fallacy, loss aversion, framing, and the inside-versus-outside view. The de-bias moves "consider the opposite", "pre-mortem", and "base-rate check" are drawn from this tradition (the pre-mortem itself is Gary Klein's). Caveat: several priming studies cited in the book did not replicate; the heuristics-and-biases findings used here are the more robust core, but effect sizes vary by context.

**Hamblin, C. L. (1970). *Fallacies*. Methuen.** The historical and logical treatment of the traditional fallacy list. Hamblin showed that many textbook "fallacies" are not formal errors but context-dependent failures of argument, and that condemning a conclusion because its argument is fallacious is itself invalid — the **fallacy fallacy** (argumentum ad logicam) that this skill's Rules section warns against.

**Halpern, D. F. (2014). *Thought and Knowledge: An Introduction to Critical Thinking* (5th ed.). Psychology Press.** The instructional frame: fallacies and biases taught as explicit, named procedures with practice, rather than as general exhortations to "be critical". Halpern's transfer evidence (e.g., Halpern 1998, *American Psychologist*) is the basis for the skill's bet that naming a failure mode changes behavior more than nagging does.

## What the evidence does not support

- **No diagnostic authority.** A cue phrase is not a diagnosis of a person or a proof of a fallacy. Humans (and models) over-apply labels; tagging is a prompt for inspection.
- **No fixed list.** The 20 + 20 split is a teaching and scanning convenience. Other catalogs number differently and disagree on boundaries (e.g., is appeal to authority always a fallacy, or only when the authority is outside their domain?).
- **Bias labeling is not debiasing.** Knowing about bias does not reliably remove it; the bias blind spot (Pronin, Lin, & Ross, 2002) means we see it in others more readily than in ourselves. That is why every tag must carry a concrete de-bias move and a revised sentence.
- **Cultural and domain variation.** Cue phrases are English-specific and skewed toward workplace and analytical writing. Treat matches in other genres as weaker signals.

## Attribution discipline

When reporting a tag, name the failure mode and the repair. Do not attribute a psychological state to the author, and do not cite these sources as if they endorsed this specific catalog. The catalog is ours; the underlying findings are theirs.
